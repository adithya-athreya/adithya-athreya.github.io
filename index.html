About
I study how firms make strategic marketing decisions that balance value creation for the different consumers they serve with their own business goals. I develop methods that help firms measure how their marketing decisions affect the consumers they serve. I study these questions in the context of healthcare and matchmaking platforms using econometric models and causal inference.

Job Market Paper
Performance Marketing and Health Outcomes in Medical Crowdfunding
with Varun Karamshetty & Paulo Albuquerque · Preparing for Management Science
▶ Abstract
Digital platforms routinely allocate marketing resources using short-term performance metrics, such as clicks or conversions, that capture consumer preference. On crowdfunding platforms, each conversion contributes to the fundraising campaign of an identifiable beneficiary and, in turn, shapes downstream outcomes for that beneficiary. Whether allocating resources based on what the paying side rewards also maximizes impact on beneficiary outcomes is therefore an empirical question. We study this question in the context of medical crowdfunding and find that performance-based marketing concentrates spend on campaigns with the best donation performance rather than those where timely fundraising most accelerates treatment and improves patient health. To quantify the effect, we develop a sequential framework, the marketing-to-health (M2H) chain, which links marketing spend to fundraising progress, fundraising to treatment timing, and treatment timing to patient health, and combines these links into the Health Return on Marketing (HROM), a marginal health return metric to guide marketing allocation decisions. Using proprietary daily campaign-level data and quasi-experimental variation in advertising exposure, we estimate each link of the chain and simulate budget-neutral allocation policies that take into account both donation performance and health outcomes. Donation-maximizing allocation is substantially misaligned with health impact: health-aware reallocation improves patient health by up to 14.6% relative to the donation-maximizing policy. Because the donation–health frontier is concave, platform managers need not abandon fundraising performance to improve patient health: a policy that trades off only 2.3% of donations improves patient health by 9.1%.

Who's Coming to Dinner? How Subscription Business Models Affect Platform Participation
with Khwan Kim & Manav Raj · Submitted to Management Science
▶ Abstract
Platforms face an important strategic choice in how they monetize user access. Subscription-only business models screen for committed users and improve retention, but may also shape how users behave once they have subscribed. We study this tradeoff using proprietary data from a global social dining platform that matches strangers into groups for weekly dinners, exploiting the staggered rollout of subscription-only pricing across 30 cities. We find that removing the pay-per-use option reduces new-user acquisition while increasing short-run retention, consistent with effective screening. At the same time, subscription-only access increases no-shows and cancellations, even among incumbent users who were already active before the policy change. These reliability failures may impose local externalities, as exposure to no-show tablemates is associated with a decrease in co-participants' experience ratings and predicts subsequent no-show behavior. The findings show that platform monetization is not only a mechanism for value capture but also a governance lever that shapes who participates, how reliably they participate, and how value is created in platform-mediated interactions.

Access Pricing and Adverse Selection in Networked Experience Goods
▶ Abstract
When firms sell shared experiences, pricing structure shapes not only who enters but the product itself. Using data from a social dining platform, we estimate a structural model that separates customers' product valuation from their outside-option quality. The central finding is that customers who value the platform most also tend to have the strongest outside alternatives: they subscribe and remain engaged, but their rich outside options make them less likely to follow through after booking. We show that interventions designed to counter subscription's reliability costs can backfire in the presence of this adverse selection.
